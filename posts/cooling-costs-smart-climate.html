Cutting Cooling Costs with Smart Climate Control

In a warm climate, cooling is often one of the biggest parts of the electricity bill. Smarter control helps you use less of it.

By Island IntegrationNov 2, 20265 min read

Start with a smart thermostat

A connected thermostat lets you set schedules and adjust temperatures from your phone. The savings come from not cooling empty rooms, which is easy to forget without automation. Check that the thermostat is compatible with your air conditioning system, since many split and multi-zone systems use different controls than a typical North American furnace and AC.

This compatibility point catches a lot of people. Ductless mini-splits, common in Bahamian homes, are usually controlled by a handheld remote rather than by thermostat wiring in the wall. Bringing them under central control means an interface module for that brand of unit or a controller that speaks to the indoor heads directly. Ducted systems with a conventional thermostat are simpler. Either way, confirm how the unit is controlled before buying anything.

Use schedules and presets

  • Raise the setpoint when no one is home.
  • Pre-cool before you arrive, using remote access.
  • Create “Away” and “Sleep” presets that apply to the whole home in one press.

The point of a preset is that it is one action rather than a tour of the house. An “Away” button by the front door that raises every setpoint, drops the shades on the sunny side and turns off the lights is more likely to be used than five separate thermostats. In a second home, a “Vacant” mode can hold the house at a moderate temperature for humidity control, and bring it back to comfortable the morning you fly in.

Humidity is half the problem

In the Bahamas, comfort is as much about moisture as temperature. A house that is cool but damp feels clammy, and a house left closed and unconditioned for weeks invites mould on furnishings, leather and electronics. An oversized air conditioner makes this worse: it cools the room quickly, then shuts off before it has run long enough to pull moisture out of the air. Smart control cannot fix an oversized unit, but it can keep the system running in a steady pattern rather than short, hard bursts, and watch humidity as well as temperature where the equipment reports it. Our article on salt air and humidity covers what that moisture does to equipment over time.

Think in zones

Rarely does every room need the same temperature at the same time. Where the equipment allows it, zoning lets bedrooms, living areas and guest suites be managed independently.

A typical pattern is to cool the living areas during the day and let the bedrooms drift, then reverse it in the evening. Guest suites can sit at a holding temperature until guests are expected. Homes with mini-splits in every room are already zoned by their nature; the missing piece is usually the coordination, so that the whole house follows one plan instead of relying on whoever last touched a remote.

Reduce the heat load

The easiest cooling is the cooling you don’t need. Keeping direct sun out of rooms with automated shades reduces the load on your air conditioning, and good insulation and sealing help too.

Large glass walls facing the water look wonderful and act as radiators in the afternoon. Motorized shades that track the sun and lower themselves on the exposed side of the house during the hottest hours take a real bite out of the load, without anyone having to remember. Ceiling fans let you hold a slightly higher setpoint and feel just as comfortable. Concrete-block construction holds heat and releases it slowly into the evening, so a house that is pre-cooled late in the afternoon stays comfortable longer than one that is cooled hard after dark.

What to look for

  • Compatibility with your actual equipment, confirmed by brand and model before purchase.
  • Local control that still works when the internet is down. A thermostat that depends on a cloud service becomes a wall ornament during an outage.
  • Humidity sensing, or at least the ability to add it.
  • Integration with the rest of the house, so climate can respond to occupancy, shades and schedules rather than living in its own app.
  • Reporting that shows run time, so you can see whether changes are helping.

Common mistakes

  • Setting the “Away” temperature so high that the house takes hours to recover and the equipment runs flat out to catch up.
  • Turning the system off entirely in a closed house for weeks and coming back to mildew.
  • Adding a smart thermostat to a system it cannot properly control, so it either does nothing or fights the unit’s own controls.
  • Relying on five separate apps for five separate units.
  • Buying new hardware before checking filters, refrigerant and ductwork. Dirty filters and leaking ducts waste more than any schedule can save.

How we approach it

We look at what is already installed and how it is controlled, then decide the simplest way to bring it under one plan. In many homes that is an interface for the existing mini-splits rather than new equipment. We set up schedules that match how the house is really used, tie climate into the same scenes that run lighting and shades, and make sure everything keeps working locally if the connection drops. Smart climate control is most useful when it is part of the whole home, and our Control4 smart home automation work is usually where the pieces come together.

Set realistic expectations

Savings depend on your equipment, building, habits and electricity rates, so we do not promise a specific percentage. Ask for a review of your current setup before you buy new hardware.

The homes that see the most benefit are the ones with big exposures to the sun, rooms that sit empty for long stretches, and owners who were cooling the whole house around the clock because there was no easy alternative.

Frequently asked questions

Can my existing mini-splits be made smart?

Usually, yes. Most brands can be controlled through an interface module or a compatible controller, and the indoor units stay exactly as they are. The right approach depends on the brand and model, so tell us what you have.

What temperature should an empty house be held at?

Warm enough to save energy, cool enough to keep humidity in check. The right figure depends on the house and season; a sensible holding setpoint with humidity monitoring beats switching the system off.

Will this work when the power or internet is out?

A well-designed system keeps its schedules and local control through an internet outage. During a power outage the air conditioning is off regardless, unless the property has a generator; see our guide to backup power for what to protect.
