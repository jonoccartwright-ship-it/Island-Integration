Keeping Your Network and Cameras Running in a Power Outage

Power interruptions are a fact of life in many parts of the Bahamas. A little planning keeps the equipment that matters online or shuts it down safely.

By Island IntegrationNov 2, 20265 min read

Decide what really needs power

Trying to back up everything is expensive. Most homes get the best value by protecting a short list:

  • The modem or fiber terminal and the router or gateway.
  • The main network switch and Wi-Fi access points (especially if they are powered over Ethernet).
  • The camera recorder and the cameras that cover entrances.
  • The home automation controller, so scenes and schedules resume cleanly.

Everything else can usually wait. A television that loses power simply turns off. A recorder that loses power while writing to its drive, or a controller that drops out halfway through a scene, is a different story. The question to ask of each device is what happens if this stops without warning? If the honest answer is “nothing much”, it does not need to be on battery. In a second home that sits empty for months, the list is shorter and matters more: if the connection and the entrance cameras stay up, you know the state of the house.

What a UPS does

An uninterruptible power supply holds the equipment up for a period of time when mains power drops, and conditions the power that comes in. Runtime depends on the battery size and the load. Add up the wattage of what you plan to connect, leave headroom, and ask for the expected runtime at that load rather than assuming a number. A UPS also gives equipment time to shut down properly, which protects storage and configuration.

Many interruptions are short: a flicker, a few minutes, occasionally an hour. A UPS sized for the network alone will often ride through those entirely. Longer outages are where it hands over to a generator or shuts the equipment down in an orderly way.

What to look for

  • Runtime at your load, not the headline capacity on the box.
  • Output quality. Some power supplies, particularly in network equipment and recorders, behave better on a clean sine-wave output. Ask whether the unit suits what it will feed.
  • Automatic voltage regulation. Brownouts are common before and after an outage. A UPS that corrects voltage without switching to battery saves the battery for when it is really needed.
  • Monitoring. A management port lets the UPS report when it has gone to battery, how much runtime remains and when the battery needs replacing.
  • Replaceable batteries. Heat shortens battery life, and most equipment closets here are warm.

Power over Ethernet helps

When access points and cameras draw power from a single switch, one UPS behind that switch can keep many devices alive. This is a good reason to plan the cabling and switch location with backup power in mind. See also Wi-Fi in large concrete homes.

The flip side is that PoE devices add to the load on that switch, and therefore on the UPS, so count them when you size the battery. Planning this is part of system design and structured cabling: a central rack with one well-sized UPS is easier to protect than equipment scattered through the house.

Generators and transfer switches

If your property has a generator, a properly installed transfer switch is what connects it safely to your electrical system. Even with a generator, a UPS is still worthwhile: it bridges the short gap while the generator starts, and smooths the changeover. Generators and fuel handling should be installed and operated by qualified people following the manufacturer’s guidance.

Generator power is not always as clean as mains power, and some UPS units respond by flipping between generator and battery. Ask the installer to test the UPS under generator power once everything is in, and to adjust its sensitivity if needed. It is a short check that avoids an unpleasant surprise during a long outage.

Don’t forget the internet itself

A UPS cannot keep your internet service working if your provider’s network is down. For remote properties, ask your provider about their own outage resilience and consider whether a second connection or mobile backup is appropriate for your needs.

A router with a second WAN port can switch to a cellular connection automatically and back again when the main line returns. On the Family Islands, where repairs can take time, that failover is often the difference between seeing the house and not. It is one of the things we consider as part of professional Wi-Fi and networking.

Common mistakes

  • Plugging equipment into the surge-only outlets on the UPS instead of the battery-backed ones.
  • Adding devices over the years until the runtime quietly shrinks to a couple of minutes.
  • Never replacing the battery. Have it checked after a few years, sooner if it lives in a hot closet.
  • Backing up the router but not the fiber terminal, which is often mounted elsewhere in the house.
  • Putting the UPS on the floor of a utility room that can take on water during a storm.
  • Treating the UPS as surge protection. It is not a substitute for a whole-home surge device at the panel, as covered in our guide to preparing for hurricane season.

What it costs to get wrong

A recorder that loses power abruptly can corrupt its storage, and the footage you needed is usually the footage that is gone. A controller that drops out can leave lights, shades or a gate in whatever state they were in. If you are abroad, each of those becomes a site visit, when a planned shutdown would have cost nothing. See also securing a second home from abroad.

How we approach it

We start with the equipment list and the real load, then the site: where the rack lives, whether there is a generator, how long outages typically last there and whether anyone is on the property. From that we size the backup, place it where it is dry and ventilated, and connect it so it reports its state. Clients on our support and remote monitoring plans have battery health checked routinely, so a tired battery is replaced before the storm that needs it.

Frequently asked questions

How long will a UPS keep my Wi-Fi running?

It depends entirely on the load and the battery. A small network on a modest UPS might run for an hour or more; the same UPS feeding a loaded PoE switch might manage a fraction of that. Ask for the runtime at your load, and assume it will shrink as the battery ages.

Do I still need a UPS if I have a generator?

Yes. The generator takes a few seconds to start and the transfer switch to change over, and that gap is enough to reboot a rack. The UPS covers it, and gives you a clean shutdown if the generator fails to start.
