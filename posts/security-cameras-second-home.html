Securing a Second Home from Abroad

Many Bahamian homes are second homes, empty for weeks or months at a time. A well-planned system gives you eyes on the property and peace of mind.

By Island IntegrationNov 2, 20265 min read

Think in layers

Security works best as a combination: deterrence, detection and a way to respond. A camera on its own does not stop anything; it records what happened. The value comes from the layers working together.

  • Deterrence: visible cameras and exterior lighting that comes on with movement.
  • Detection: cameras with motion alerts and door or window sensors.
  • Response: a trusted local contact, caretaker or monitoring service who can check the property.

For a second home the third layer matters most. If an alert arrives at 3 a.m. in London or Toronto and nobody in Nassau can go and look, it has limited value. Decide who that person is before you decide which cameras to buy, and give them a way to see the same footage you do.

Camera placement

Cover entrances, driveways and the areas around the building, rather than trying to see everything. Use outdoor-rated cameras, and be aware of the effects of salt air on exposed equipment. Point cameras at your own property, and be mindful of your neighbors’ privacy and any local rules.

A few placement habits that pay off:

  • Mount cameras high enough to be out of easy reach, but not so high that faces become the tops of heads.
  • Put a camera on each approach to the house, including the dock or beach side if the property has one. Many Bahamian homes are easier to reach from the water than the road.
  • Cover the pool deck and any outbuildings, generator enclosure or utility area where equipment is stored.
  • Avoid pointing a camera directly into the rising or setting sun; the image will wash out for an hour or two each day.
  • Choose a stainless or marine-grade mount and sealed cabling on any camera facing the sea. Exposed wire and plated screws corrode fast in salt air.

Wired cameras on a Power over Ethernet connection are the dependable choice: no batteries, continuous recording to a local recorder, and a single cable that is easy to protect with backup power. Battery cameras suit a quick retrofit, but someone has to be there to charge them.

Reliable remote access

Remote viewing depends on the internet connection and on power. Plan for battery backup at the router and recorder, and test that alerts reach your phone when you are in another country and time zone.

BPL outages are a fact of life, and an empty house has nobody to reset anything. A UPS on the network equipment and recorder keeps cameras recording through short outages and, just as important, lets everything restart cleanly when power returns. If the property has a generator, make sure the network rack is on a generator-fed circuit. For longer outages, the recorder should keep recording locally even when the internet is down, so you can review what happened once the connection is back.

A cellular backup for the internet connection gives you a view when the main link is down. Our support and remote monitoring service watches the network itself, so we often know a site has gone offline before the owner does.

Lighting and occupancy simulation

Scheduled or varied lighting makes a house look lived in. Pairing this with lighting control is simple once the system is in place.

The trick is variation. A porch light that comes on at exactly 7 p.m. every night and goes off at exactly 11 p.m. tells anyone watching that nobody is home. A lighting control system can shift the timing with sunset, turn rooms on and off in a realistic sequence, and leave the kitchen on later some nights than others. Add motion-triggered exterior lights and a few interior lamps on the same system, and the house behaves the way an occupied home does.

Access control

Smart locks let you grant a caretaker or service provider temporary access without handing out keys, and review who came and went. Choose hardware suited to the environment and the door.

In practice this means a code for the housekeeper that works on her usual days, a one-time code for the pool technician, and a notification on your phone when either is used. If a contractor needs access for a week, the code expires on its own. No keys get copied.

Lock hardware takes a beating near the sea. Look for finishes rated for coastal use, and plan on the lock being part of regular servicing. Where a door is wood and the house sits empty through the humid summer, the door can swell enough to stop a deadbolt from throwing; a camera on that door tells you whether the lock actually engaged. For gates, garages and pedestrian entries, our security cameras and access control work covers the whole property, not just the front door.

Common mistakes we see

  • Relying on Wi-Fi cameras in a concrete house. Block walls weaken the signal, and a camera that drops offline is a camera that misses the event. See why Wi-Fi struggles in concrete homes.
  • No backup power. The first outage takes the whole system down, and it may not come back on its own.
  • Too many alerts. If every palm frond triggers a notification, you will mute the app within a week. Motion zones and person detection keep alerts meaningful.
  • Nobody local. Alerts with no response plan are just a record of what you lost.
  • Forgetting hurricane season. From June to November, know how the system will be secured and who will check it after a storm. Our hurricane season checklist covers the details.

How we approach it

We start with how the house is used: who visits, when, and from which direction. From that we lay out camera coverage, decide where the recorder and network live, and plan the backup power. You, your caretaker and our support team each see what you need to see, and we test the alerts from abroad before we call the job done.

Frequently asked questions

Can I see my cameras from overseas?

Yes, provided the internet connection and power at the house are reliable. That is why we spend as much time on the network and backup power as on the cameras themselves.

Do I need a monitoring company as well?

Not always. Many owners rely on a caretaker or property manager who receives the same alerts. A monitored service adds a guaranteed response, which some insurers and some owners prefer.

How much footage is kept?

It depends on the recorder’s storage and the number of cameras. We size storage so you can be away for an extended period and still review the full history when something happens.
