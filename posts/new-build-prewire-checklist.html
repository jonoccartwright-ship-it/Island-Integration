Building in the Bahamas? Your Smart Home Pre-Wire Checklist

The cheapest time to add wiring is before the walls are finished. A short checklist now saves expensive work later.

By Island IntegrationNov 2, 20265 min read

Involve your integrator early

Bring a technology designer into the project when the floor plans are still flexible. Early involvement lets cable routes, equipment locations and ceiling layouts be coordinated with the architect, builder and electrician. In concrete construction, conduit often needs to be placed before pouring.

This matters more in the Bahamas than in a wood-framed home. Most houses here are built from concrete block with poured slabs and beams, and once those are cured there is no easy way to fish a cable through them. A cable that would take an hour to add in a drywall home can mean cutting chases, patching and repainting, or settling for surface-mounted trunking that nobody wanted. The right time to decide where the equipment room, access points, speakers and cameras go is while the plans are still on paper. Our system design and structured cabling work starts at that stage for exactly this reason.

A good first meeting covers how you plan to live in the house: which rooms will have TVs, where you will sit outside, and whether the property will sit empty for part of the year. Those answers shape the wiring more than any product choice does.

The checklist

  • Network: Ethernet cable to every planned access point location, TV location, office and any camera position. Plan ceiling-mounted access points for each area. Two cables to a TV or desk location cost almost nothing extra and give you a spare.
  • Equipment room: a cool, ventilated space for the network, audio and control equipment, with enough power and room to grow. A dedicated closet with a return-air path or its own small split unit is far better than a cupboard under the stairs.
  • Conduit: spare conduit between floors and to key rooms so future cables can be pulled without opening walls. Include a pull string and cap both ends.
  • TVs and projectors: power and data at the display, and a recessed box where you want a clean wall mount. See our TV mounting guide. For a cinema or media room, plan the projector position, screen wall and speaker locations together rather than one at a time.
  • Audio: speaker cable to ceilings and walls for indoor and outdoor audio zones. Cable for the patio, pool deck and garden should be run before landscaping and paving, not after.
  • Lighting: confirm the wiring needs of your lighting control system with your electrician. Centralized systems need the circuits routed to a panel location; keypad-based systems need the keypad boxes and any low-voltage wiring in place before the walls are plastered.
  • Shades: power or low-voltage wiring at window heads, and a pocket or recess if you want a clean look. Motorized shades are one of the hardest things to add well after the fact, because the pocket has to be built into the ceiling detail.
  • Security: cable and power to exterior camera locations, gates and entries. Gate operators, intercoms and keypads usually need both power and data at the gate, which means a long conduit run that is easy during site work and painful afterwards. See our security cameras and access control page for what is typically needed at each entry.
  • Climate: low-voltage wiring from the equipment room or panel to each thermostat location, so the air conditioning can be controlled from the same system as everything else.
  • Power quality: a circuit for the equipment room that can be fed from a UPS or generator transfer switch, so the network and control system stay up through the short BPL outages that are part of life here.

Common mistakes we see on new builds

Most of the problems we get called to fix after a build are planning gaps that were cheap to avoid at the time.

  • One cable per room. A single Ethernet run to a living room has to serve the TV, a streaming box, a games console and a Wi-Fi access point. Run more than you think you need.
  • Access points in the wrong place. An access point tucked in a closet or behind a concrete beam covers a fraction of what the same unit does on a ceiling in the open.
  • No equipment room at all. Gear ends up in a garage or an outdoor utility space where heat, humidity and dust shorten its life.
  • Outdoor runs added last. By the time someone thinks about pool speakers or a camera on the dock, the pavers are down and the planting is in.

Leave room to grow

Technology changes faster than buildings do. Extra cable and conduit cost little during construction and give you options for years.

A practical rule is to run cable to any location where you can imagine wanting a device in the next decade, even if nothing goes there on day one. A spare conduit from the equipment room to the roof gives you a path for a future antenna or weather station. Conduit to the dock, garage or pool house is nearly impossible to add later without trenching through finished landscaping. On larger properties, fiber between buildings is worth considering: it is immune to the lightning-induced surges that travel along copper during summer storms.

Document everything

Photograph the walls before they close and keep a labeled plan of every cable. It will help whoever services the home in the future.

Good documentation includes a floor plan marked with each cable’s label and endpoint, photographs of the open walls with a tape measure in frame, and a list of what is at each end. On a second home managed from abroad, this record lets a technician answer a question remotely instead of making a site visit, and it is the foundation for ongoing support and remote monitoring once the system is live.

Frequently asked questions

Can I pre-wire for a smart home without deciding on the system yet?

Yes. Structured cabling, conduit and equipment-room planning are largely the same regardless of which control platform you choose later. The exceptions are lighting and shades, where the wiring method depends on the system, so decide those before the electrical rough-in.

Is wireless good enough that I can skip the cabling?

Not in a concrete home. Wireless devices still need a wired access point nearby, and TVs, cameras and the main control equipment should be on a cable. Wireless is for the devices that cannot be wired, not a replacement for the backbone.

How early is early enough?

Before the slab is poured is ideal, because that is when under-floor conduit is placed. Failing that, before the block walls are plastered. After that, every option gets more expensive.
