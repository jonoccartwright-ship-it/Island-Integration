Why Wi-Fi Struggles in Large Concrete Homes (and How to Fix It)

If the Wi-Fi is strong in the room with the router and weak everywhere else, the building is probably part of the problem.

By Island IntegrationNov 2, 20265 min read

Why walls matter

Wi-Fi signals weaken as they pass through dense materials. Concrete block, poured concrete and walls with steel reinforcement absorb and reflect a lot of signal, and higher-frequency bands lose more strength than lower ones. Mesh “boosters” that talk to each other wirelessly can struggle because the link between them is itself weakened by the walls.

Almost every home in the Bahamas is built this way, for good reason: block and reinforced concrete stand up to hurricanes. Add a concrete roof slab, tile over concrete floors and hurricane-rated glass with metallic coatings, and the building behaves like a series of separate boxes as far as radio signal is concerned.

The result is familiar: full bars in the room with the router, one bar in the master bedroom, nothing on the pool deck. Turning up the router’s power does not help much, because your phone has to reach back through the same walls with its own small radio.

Wire the access points

The most reliable approach is to connect several wired access points to a central switch using Ethernet cable, and place them where people actually use the network. Each access point serves a smaller area with a strong signal. For a primer, read how a wireless access point works.

The principle is simple: let the cable do the hard work of getting through the walls, and let Wi-Fi do only the last few metres into the room. Every access point gets its own full-speed connection to the switch, so adding more makes the network better, not slower. With a wireless mesh, every extra node shares the same shrinking pool of signal.

A well-designed system also lets your devices move between access points as you walk through the house without dropping a call or a stream. That clean hand-off is one of the main differences between professional Wi-Fi and networking and a box of consumer mesh units.

Placement beats power

  • Mount access points on ceilings, in open areas, rather than hidden in cabinets or behind TVs.
  • Plan for a different access point per floor or wing rather than one for the whole house.
  • Cover outdoor areas deliberately with outdoor-rated access points.
  • Keep the main switch and router in a ventilated, cool location.

Ceiling mounting matters more in concrete homes than elsewhere. A ceiling-mounted access point looks down into a room with nothing in the way, whereas one tucked into a cabinet starts with a wall in front of it. Where the ceiling is a concrete slab, the cable needs to arrive through conduit, one more reason to plan this during construction.

Outdoors is its own job. The pool deck, dock, guest cottage and garden are where a lot of the living happens, and a signal leaking out of a window will not reach them reliably. Outdoor-rated access points under an eave or on a pergola cover these areas properly. Mounts and cable entries need attention because salt air and humidity find every unsealed gap.

Power and cabling

Power over Ethernet delivers data and power over one cable, which avoids needing an outlet at every access point and simplifies battery backup. If you are building or renovating, run the cable while the walls are open.

Because every access point is powered from the switch, one UPS at the rack keeps the whole network running through a BPL outage. That matters for cameras and the alarm, and so that everything comes back cleanly when power returns. A properly built rack, with labelled cabling, a managed switch and ventilation, is the foundation every other system in the house sits on. Our system design and structured cabling service handles this from the pre-wire stage, and the pre-wire checklist lists what to run before the walls close.

For an existing home, cable can usually be routed through attic spaces, closets, soffits or surface conduit without major disruption. It takes planning, but it is a one-time job.

Internet speed is a separate question

A fast internet plan does not fix weak coverage inside the home, and good coverage does not make a slow plan faster. Diagnose the two separately: test the speed at the router, then at the places you actually use the connection.

If the speed at the router is close to what you pay for and falls off a cliff two rooms away, the building is the problem and the fix is inside the house. If the speed is poor even next to the router, the issue is the service or the modem, and no number of access points will change it. Upgrading the plan is often the first thing people try, and often the wrong one.

Common mistakes

  • Buying a stronger router. Power is not the problem; walls are.
  • Wireless mesh in a block house. The nodes cannot hear each other well enough to be useful.
  • Hiding the equipment. An access point in a closet, behind a TV or inside a metal cabinet is working at a disadvantage from the start.
  • One access point for the whole house. Several smaller cells always beat one loud one.
  • Forgetting the outdoors. If the family spends evenings by the pool, the network should reach the pool.
  • No backup power. The network is the first thing to go in an outage and the last thing anyone thinks to protect.

Frequently asked questions

How many access points does a house need?

It depends on the layout and construction, not just the square footage. A long single-storey block house with an outdoor living area often needs more than a compact two-storey one. A walk-through tells us quickly.

Can I keep my provider’s router?

Usually the provider’s box stays as the modem and the home network is built behind it with its own router, switch and access points, so you can change providers without redoing the house.

Will this work on the Family Islands?

Yes. The design is the same; the internet service may be different. Where the connection is slower, good coverage inside the house matters even more, because you want every bit of the bandwidth you have to reach the places you use it.
